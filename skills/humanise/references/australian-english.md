# Australian English reference

This reference covers the three layers of Australian English to apply: spelling, vocabulary, and idiom. The level configured for this skill is **spelling + vocab + light idiom, professional register**.

## Principles

Australian English sits close to British English on spelling but has its own vocabulary and a distinct cadence. In professional contexts, it reads as clean British-adjacent English with occasional Australianisms — not heavy slang. The goal is prose that an Australian reader recognises as theirs without an American or British reader stumbling.

**Register awareness matters more than rules.** "Reckon" is fine in an internal email, wrong in a tender document. "Happy to run through it" lands professionally almost anywhere in Australia. "No worries" lands in emails between colleagues but reads too casual in a client-facing proposal. Use the document's register to dial the idiom up or down.

## 1. Spelling

### -ise, not -ize
Australia uses -ise endings universally (unlike British English, which accepts both Oxford's -ize and -ise).

- organise, not organize
- recognise, realise, analyse, optimise, prioritise, minimise, maximise, utilise, authorise, characterise, summarise, customise, standardise, finalise, visualise

### -our, not -or
- colour, favour, honour, labour, behaviour, neighbour, harbour, flavour, humour, rumour, armour, endeavour, rigour, vigour, vapour, tumour, odour

Note: *glamour* keeps the u; but *glamorous* drops it.

### -re, not -er (for nouns ending in consonant-re)
- centre, metre, litre, theatre, calibre, fibre, sombre, lustre, sceptre, spectre

Note: *meter* (as in parking meter, measuring device) keeps -er; *metre* is the unit of length.

### Doubled consonants before suffixes
- travelling, cancelling, modelling, labelling, signalling, totalling, pencilling, jewellery, counselling, fuelled

### -ce, not -se (for nouns where verb is -se)
- licence (noun) / license (verb)
- practice (noun) / practise (verb)
- advice (noun) / advise (verb)
- defence, offence, pretence

### Other specific spellings
- cheque (not check, for banking)
- grey (not gray)
- programme (for broadcast/performance), but program (for computing)
- enrolment, instalment, skilful, fulfil (one l)
- storey (of a building), story (narrative)
- tyre (wheel), tire (fatigue)
- kerb (footpath edge), curb (restraint)
- aluminium (not aluminum)
- dialogue, catalogue, analogue (keep the -ue)
- judgement (but "judgment" acceptable in legal contexts)

### Time and date
- "Fortnight" is standard in AU for two weeks — fine in professional writing ("a fortnight's notice", "fortnightly report").
- Dates: DD/MM/YYYY or "24 April 2026" (day before month).
- "This arvo" is casual-only; "this afternoon" is neutral.

## 3. Idiom — light, professional register

Phrases that land in Australian business writing without tipping into slang:

### Fine at most professional registers
- "happy to..." (happy to run through it, happy to set up a call)
- "keen to..." (keen to get this moving, keen to hear thoughts)
- "sort (something) out"
- "across (the detail / the issue)" — meaning "fully briefed on"
- "quick one" (as in "quick one for you")
- "flick through" (send across, informal-professional)
- "heads up"
- "give it a crack" / "give it a go"
- "reckon" — fine in internal emails, Slack, informal notes; avoid in formal client documents
- "fair enough"
- "no worries" — fine in emails between colleagues, not in formal client-facing prose
- "gets me thinking"
- "touch on" (as in "a doc that touches on X")
- "work through"
- "run past (you)"
- "let me know how you'd like to play it"
- "happy days"
- "sing out if..." (as in "sing out if anything's unclear")

### Use sparingly; depends on audience
- "chuck (something) in / through"
- "pop (something) together"
- "heaps" (as intensifier — fine in casual business, avoid in formal documents)
- "dodgy" (only for genuinely informal contexts)
- "stuff up" (verb: to stuff up = to mess up; fine internally)
- "a bit of a..." (hedged construction — "a bit of a mess", "a bit of a stretch")

### Avoid in business writing
Terms that tip into slang or bogan register and will jar in most professional contexts:
- g'day, mate, cobber, sheila, bloke (as address)
- arvo (informal only), brekkie, servo, bottle-o
- "she'll be right" (too casual)
- "fair dinkum", "dead-set", "bloody oath"
- "yeah nah" / "nah yeah" (casual-only)

## 4. Calibrating to the document

Ask: **who's the reader, and how formal is this?**

- **Internal email to a peer**: full use of Section 3 "Fine" idiom; light use of "Use sparingly"; AU spelling + vocab throughout.
- **Email to an external partner or client**: spelling + vocab, idiom only the safest ("happy to", "keen to", "across", "quick one", "sort out"); drop "reckon", "no worries", "chuck".
- **Formal proposal, report, tender**: spelling + vocab only; strip idiom almost entirely; sentences remain in professional register throughout. AU English shows up in "colour", "centre", "-ise", "while" (not "whilst") — not in phrasing.
- **Executive / board document**: spelling + vocab, minimal idiom; aim for clean British-Australian professional English. Voice signatures still transfer (rhythm, structure, argument shape) but lexical idiom is pared back.

## 5. Common traps

- **"Whilst" vs "while"**: Australians generally use "while". "Whilst" is present but reads slightly affected/British; prefer "while" unless the user's profile clearly favours "whilst".
- **"Amongst" vs "among"**: same — "among" is more natural in modern AU.
- **"Towards" vs "toward"**: "towards" is AU-natural.
- **"Got" vs "gotten"**: AU uses "got" as past participle ("I've got"), not "gotten". "Gotten" is an American marker.
- **"Could care less" is wrong**; the phrase is "couldn't care less".
- **Collective nouns**: AU tends toward singular agreement ("the team is meeting", not "the team are meeting"), though plural is also heard and acceptable.
- **Serial (Oxford) comma**: AU style is mixed. Use where it aids clarity; don't impose it dogmatically. Match the user's profile if it reveals a preference.
- **Quotation marks**: AU uses single quotes for main quotes, double for quotes-within-quotes (reverse of US convention). But double quotes are now widespread in AU business writing too; match the source document's convention.

## Quick summary (relocated from SKILL.md Step 4)

- Spelling: -ise (not -ize), -our (colour, favour), -re (centre, metre), -ll- (travelling, cancelled), programme/program per context, etc.
- Vocabulary: mum (not mom), footpath (not sidewalk), uni (not college), rubbish (not trash), etc.
- Light idiom at professional register: "happy to", "keen to", "across" (as in "across the detail"), "sort out", "reckon" (sparingly, only where register permits), "fair enough", "quick one". Avoid "g'day", "mate", "arvo", "heaps" in business documents unless the voice profile clearly supports it and the audience is internal/peer.
